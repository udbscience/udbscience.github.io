Three numbers that need honest labels

GOOGL & AMZN · the GAAP trailing EPS is not the operating story

Both companies’ trailing GAAP EPS is dominated by 2026 non-cash equity revaluations — Alphabet’s $37.7B (Q1) and $98.0B (Q2) gains, partly reversed by an $8.7B Q3 loss; Amazon’s Anthropic marks, $12.3B (Q1) and $50.5B (Q2). The table uses disclosed ex-gain core EPS for both, with the unadjusted figures above. Read the +38% and +6% on the core numbers; the GAAP figures would flatter both by roughly $300 and $170 of “value” that no operating business earned.

FISV · +317% — superseded, not updated

The Oct 29 verdict paper (Fiserv Feb 10 and Aug 6, 2026 releases; Zacks; WallStreetZen) concludes the $201.12 was a model artifact: trailing GAAP EPS has since fallen to $5.21, FY2026 guidance was cut to $7.20–$7.40, organic revenue is flat to −1%, and consensus long-term growth is 1.4%. The Nov 3 Q3 — adjusted EPS $1.75 vs $1.72 consensus, guidance affirmed, not raised — does not overturn that verdict. One beat is explicitly not enough.

WMT · −57% — a basis gap, not an error

The model uses trailing GAAP EPS of $2.73. The market pays ~40× on adjusted earnings and a growth story. The model is saying the multiple, not the business, is the risk.

GE Vernova is the exception in the table. Its $697 value and $523 buy threshold (25% margin of safety) come from the Oct 8 two-stage free-cash-flow DCF, not this EPS method. At $832.83 it remains above its IV and well above the buy threshold.

This is a screening model, not a recommendation. It rewards trailing earnings and punishes high multiples by construction — which is exactly why the footnotes above matter as much as the ranking.

How to read this

Start with the ranking, then interrogate it. Broadridge (+76%) and Netflix (+42%) are the cleanest “wonderful business, plausible discount” reads, unchanged in value since Oct 20 — only their prices moved. Alphabet (+38% on core earnings) is a larger discount than Oct 20’s +17%, driven by the price fall, not by the paper gains. Amazon (+6%) and Union Pacific (−4%) sit essentially at fair value: no margin of safety, no obvious overpayment. Apple (−7%) screens less rich than before, on a genuinely better fiscal year. At the other end, Walmart and Johnson & Johnson screen rich on trailing GAAP earnings — the model is flagging multiple risk, not saying the businesses are broken.
